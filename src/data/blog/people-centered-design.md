When we talk about human-centered design in technology, we often focus narrowly on user experience: making interfaces intuitive and workflows efficient.

But truly people-centered technology asks a broader question. What does this product do to the community, culture, and system around the person using it?

## The interface is only one layer

A clear interface matters. It reduces uncertainty, helps people recover from mistakes, and makes the next action obvious. But an interface can be polished while the product behind it remains poorly matched to real life.

In Ethiopia, where technology adoption is growing quickly but connectivity, device access, and digital confidence vary widely, that distinction matters. A product designed only for the ideal device, connection, or user will exclude many of the people it claims to serve.

## Design for the surrounding reality

People-centered work starts by understanding the whole setting: who has access, who makes decisions, what happens when the network fails, and how the product changes an existing way of working.

That means treating low bandwidth, shared devices, clear language, and recoverable workflows as product requirements rather than edge cases. It also means measuring whether the product improves the outcome it was built for—not only whether people can complete a screen.

The best technology does not call attention to its architecture. It quietly helps someone do useful work, in the conditions they actually have.
